    THE TWO-MINUTE LEGAL OVERVIEW

    Why accessibility matters—and what the law asks us to do

    Students with disabilities must have an equally effective opportunity to learn, participate, and demonstrate what they know. Accessible course materials help make that possible.

    For faculty, the practical starting point is to create and select accessible materials, check what students actually receive, correct barriers within your control, and work with campus support when more help is needed. The college has legal responsibilities too: faculty are not expected to solve every vendor or technical problem alone.

    Which laws guide this work?

    Think of three overlapping legal frameworks—not dozens of separate laws you must memorize:

    1. ADA Title II: Public colleges must provide nondiscriminatory access and effective communication. Its web accessibility rule incorporates WCAG 2.1 AA as a technical standard for covered web content and mobile apps, subject to its provisions.
    2. Section 504: Federally funded colleges must not discriminate on the basis of disability and must address access to educational programs.
    3. California Government Code § 11135: Adds disability nondiscrimination protections for programs receiving state financial assistance.

    WCAG is a technical standard, not a fourth statute. Section 508 may inform procurement and technical practices, but should not be confused with the main direct Title II requirement for public college course sites.

    See the legal explanations and citations → · Browse original sources →

    What does “equally effective” mean?

    Equal access does not always require identical formats. A different format can provide an equally effective opportunity to receive the information and participate in the learning activity.

    Ask: Can the student get the essential content, complete the same learning task, and participate with appropriate timeliness, independence, and privacy?

    Example: A transcript may capture a math lecture's spoken words but miss the graph the instructor draws. That visual mathematical meaning must also be communicated.

    Important distinction: Providing an alternative does not automatically make an inaccessible posted PDF or webpage compliant. The Title II web rule limits when a separate conforming alternate web version can replace an inaccessible original. When possible, make the main material accessible; consult campus support about alternatives.

    What if I can't make my original material accessible?

    Start by identifying what a student needs to learn or do. Fix the original when feasible; if an alternative is needed, preserve essential information, activities, feedback, and timely access. Document the barrier and contact the appropriate campus accessibility or technology support team—especially for publisher platforms and complex media.

    Do not assume: “I posted a textbook chapter instead” or “I have auto-captions” necessarily provides the same access as your annotated notes or visual lecture. Also, reporting a barrier alone does not address a student's immediate need for access.

    Start with the legal requirement—not a wall of links

    Start here: Read the two-minute plain-language overview →. The main federal frameworks are ADA Title II and Section 504, alongside California Government Code §11135. The Title II web rule references WCAG 2.1 AA. Equally effective access is a guiding principle, but it is not a blanket permission to leave the original web material inaccessible.

    Use the situation cards below to locate a short regulatory excerpt, understand its relevance, and follow an appropriate verification path. Legal text is different from implementation advice. These summaries are educational and do not determine whether a particular course is compliant.

    1. Web and LMS content, including vendors

    Binding rule: 28 CFR § 35.200(a)(1). Exact excerpt: “directly or through contractual, licensing, or other arrangements”.

    Faculty situation: A Pearson MyLab Math, MyOpenMath, or other homework activity is embedded in Canvas. An iframe or external link does not itself remove the college's obligations. The third-party-content exception is narrower than “someone else made it.”

    Try: Request an ACR/VPAT, test a real assignment with keyboard and screen reader, record barriers, and refer vendor-controlled failures to campus support.

    Read § 35.200 · Exceptions § 35.201

    2. PDFs, handwritten notes, and LaTeX

    Binding rule: § 35.200, subject to applicable exceptions in § 35.201. Exact excerpt: “readily accessible to and usable by individuals with disabilities”.

    Faculty situation: A compiled LaTeX PDF, scanned worksheet, or annotated iPad handout is used in an active Canvas course. A PDF that looks correct or has selectable text is not necessarily usable with assistive technology.

    Try: Test math reading, tags, reading order, graphs, and the student task; consider MathML-based HTML, accessible Word equations, or properly remediated PDF. A raw .tex file alone is not a demonstrated equivalent.

    Read § 35.200 · Document exceptions

    3. Graphs and assessments

    Incorporated technical standard: WCAG 2.1 success criterion 1.1.1. Exact excerpt: “If non-text content is a test or exercise that would be invalid if presented in text”.

    Faculty situation: The question asks students to determine a vertex, trend, or asymptote. A description should provide access without automatically stating the conclusion being assessed. The special test provision does not resolve all effective-communication and equal-access obligations.

    Try: Identify the skill being assessed; supply suitable graph data, tactile representation, or equivalent task when needed; review with accessibility specialists. There is no universal WCAG 125-character alt-text limit.

    Read WCAG 1.1.1 · Complex images guide

    4. Accessible alternate versions

    Binding rule: §§ 35.202 and 35.203. Exact excerpt (§ 35.203): “substantially equivalent or greater accessibility and usability”.

    Faculty situation: A video, textbook, or alternate worksheet is offered alongside an inaccessible original. This is not an automatic blanket exception; consider the specific conditions for conforming alternate versions and equivalent facilitation.

    Try: Compare learning objectives, access timing, navigability, feedback, and student independence; consult campus accessibility personnel before declaring the arrangement sufficient.

    Read § 35.202 · Read § 35.203

    Source hierarchy: ADA Title II and Section 504 are laws; 28 CFR Part 35 is binding federal regulation; WCAG 2.1 A/AA is the technical standard incorporated into the Title II web rule; DOJ explanations, W3C tutorials, CCC resources, testing tools, and vendor ACRs serve different guidance or evidence roles. A passing automated scan does not certify full accessibility.

    Student rights and accessibility concerns

    Focus on timely access and problem-solving. Students can raise concerns when course materials or activities are inaccessible. Faculty should respond promptly and coordinate with the appropriate campus office. The information below explains available routes without suggesting that a complaint establishes a violation.

    How can a student raise an accessibility concern?

    1. Seek timely assistance: Contact the instructor, disability services (DSPS), or the college ADA/Section 504 coordinator to describe the barrier and request access.
    2. Use campus procedures if desired: Consult the college's published disability grievance process. The local procedure and contacts must be verified before this hub links to them.
    3. Contact the U.S. Department of Education Office for Civil Rights (OCR): Students may file a disability discrimination complaint without generally having to finish a campus grievance process first. OCR complaints ordinarily must be filed within 180 days of the alleged discrimination, although exceptions and special rules may apply, including rules after an institutional grievance.

    What happens next? OCR reviews the submission to determine whether it can investigate. It may seek early resolution, investigate, or close a matter depending on the facts and its procedures. Filing a complaint does not itself establish discrimination. Retaliation for exercising protected civil rights is prohibited.

    U.S. Department of Education: How to file an OCR complaint

    What might happen institutionally?

    • Review or investigation of the reported access barrier.
    • Early resolution, mediation, or a voluntary corrective-action agreement.
    • Formal agency findings and compliance monitoring where applicable.
    • For unresolved Section 504 violations, federal-funding enforcement procedures or referral for enforcement, subject to legal safeguards.
    • Litigation and court-ordered remedies; availability of monetary relief depends on the legal claim and facts.

    These are possibilities, not a required sequence or guaranteed outcome.

    How do I test my webpage for accessibility?

    New to screen readers? Start with the 10-minute screen reader guide (NVDA, Narrator, or VoiceOver) before doing this check.

    Short answer: Use a free automated scanner to identify likely problems, then manually test the actual student tasks. A scan alone cannot certify WCAG compliance.

    Choose your webpage

    Open WAVE's free URL checker and enter your public URL. Review errors, contrast warnings, and structural alerts. Investigate every finding.

    Five-minute manual check

    1. Use Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, and Space without a mouse. Can you complete the intended activity?
    2. Zoom to 200% and check readability, controls, and clipping. Test 400% reflow where applicable.
    3. Check headings, links, form labels, and visible keyboard focus.
    4. Test an important equation, graph, or dynamic result using NVDA (Windows) or VoiceOver (Mac).
    5. Check text and control contrast; then complete the real learning task, including feedback and submission.

    What should I do next? If the page fails, record the URL, the exact step, and what happened. Fix what you control, retest, and seek institutional/vendor support for unresolved barriers.

    No URL or page content is sent to this hub. Third-party testing tools have their own privacy practices; avoid submitting protected content to public scanners.

    Does my text meet color contrast requirements?

    Short answer: WCAG 2.1 AA generally requires at least 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text (at least 18 pt or 14 pt bold), and 3:1 for essential interface components and graphical objects where the non-text criterion applies.

    Try the color contrast checker

    Sample math: y = 2x + 3 · Graph labels and instructions

    For gradients, images, transparency, and text overlays, sample the actual rendered colors. This simple checker evaluates two opaque solid colors only.

    How to interpret the result

    • 4.5:1 minimum for ordinary text, including most axis labels and worksheet instructions.
    • 3:1 for qualifying large text and important graphical objects or user interface boundaries.
    • Color alone is insufficient: distinguish red and green graph curves with line styles, labels, or symbols as well as color.

    What should I do next? If contrast fails, change foreground or background colors and retest the actual page or graph. If a publisher's content fails, document it and contact campus support.

    WebAIM Contrast Checker · WCAG 1.4.3 · WCAG 1.4.11

    How do I check whether my interactive website is accessible?

    New to screen readers? Start with the 10-minute screen reader guide (NVDA, Narrator, or VoiceOver) before doing this check.

    Short answer: Test a real student activity with a keyboard and screen reader, check zoom and visual meaning, and use an automated checker to find additional issues. An AI-built site is not automatically accessible.

    Try these five checks on the published website

    1. Keyboard: Put the mouse aside. Use Tab and Shift+Tab to move; use Enter, Space, and arrow keys as appropriate. Can you finish the activity, see where focus is, and leave every control?
    2. Screen reader: On Windows, try free NVDA; on Mac, try VoiceOver. Are instructions, buttons, equations, answers, and feedback announced meaningfully?
    3. Learning task: Can someone understand graphs, instructions, errors, and changing results without relying only on color, sound, or dragging with a mouse?
    4. Zoom: Check at 200% and, where reflow applies, at a narrow 320-CSS-pixel equivalent / 400% zoom. Can students still read and operate the activity? Some inherently two-dimensional content has specific reflow exceptions.
    5. Automated scan: Try WAVE or axe. Investigate findings; neither tool can confirm full compliance.

    What are the rules for accessible headings in Word, Canvas, and PDFs?

    Short answer: Use real, built-in heading styles or tags to describe the document outline. A bold, centered, or large-font line is not automatically a heading for a screen reader.

    Five rules to remember

    1. Make headings meaningful: “Practice Problems” tells readers more than “Section B.”
    2. Use a logical hierarchy: Heading 1 (H1) is the top level; Heading 2 (H2) is a section within it; Heading 3 (H3) is a subsection. Think of an outline, not a font-size menu.
    3. Don't skip levels without a structural reason: Prefer H1 → H2 → H3, not H1 → H3 merely because H3 looks nicer. You can change the style's appearance separately.
    4. Use headings for headings only: Don't mark an entire paragraph as a heading, and don't use a heading style simply to make text prominent.
    5. Check the actual output: A visually correct page or PDF may still lack meaningful heading structure.

    A simple math lesson outline

    Document title: Exponential Functions

    Heading 1: Evaluating Exponential Functions

    Heading 2: Using a Table

    Heading 2: Using a Formula

    Heading 3: Example: Evaluate f(2)

    Heading 1: Graphing Exponential Functions

    This is an example of a standalone Word document. On a Canvas page, Canvas normally supplies the page title, so start the content sections at Heading 2 instead.

    How to apply the rules in each format

    Microsoft Word: apply actual heading styles
    1. Select the text that names a section.
    2. Go to Home → Styles and choose Heading 1, Heading 2, or Heading 3 according to the outline. The document title can use Word's Title style; it is not necessarily Heading 1, so include a meaningful Heading 1 for major sections where appropriate.
    3. Open View → Navigation Pane and select Headings. Does the outline make sense? If a heading is missing, it may only be bold text.
    4. Run Review → Check Accessibility and correct relevant findings. The checker does not replace reviewing the outline.

    Tip: Change the appearance by modifying the heading style—not by choosing the wrong heading level.

    Canvas page: start at the right level
    1. Open the page and choose Edit in the Rich Content Editor.
    2. Select a section label and use the Paragraph/Heading format menu. Choose Heading 2 for a main content section and Heading 3 for a subsection; use Heading 4 only for a subsection of Heading 3.
    3. Canvas typically provides the page title as the top-level heading, so avoid adding another H1 in the body solely for styling.
    4. Check the published page's heading order, then use the Rich Content Editor Accessibility Checker for additional detectable issues.

    Note: The exact menu label varies by Canvas version and editor configuration.

    PDF: verify heading tags, not just appearance
    1. Best starting point: Apply heading styles in Word or another source document before exporting.
    2. Export using an option that preserves document structure tags; avoid printing to PDF if that removes tags.
    3. In a PDF accessibility/tag inspection tool (such as Adobe Acrobat Pro), inspect the Tags tree. Real headings should be tagged <H1>, <H2>, <H3>, etc., in a logical sequence.
    4. Check that the reading order and heading navigation match the document. A scanned PDF or a PDF with visually large text but no heading tags needs remediation.

    Important: A PDF's title metadata and its visible title are different from heading tags. Text selection or OCR alone does not establish correct structure.

    Quick self-check

    Ask: “If I could only see a list of headings, would I understand the organization of this lesson?” If yes, and those headings are represented by real styles or tags, you're on the right track. This is one accessibility check, not a full document audit.

    Need help? If a PDF has no tags, an exported file loses its heading structure, or your platform doesn't offer the needed heading levels, contact campus accessibility support. W3C: Headings tutorial · WCAG 1.3.1.

    Is my Canvas page accessible?

    Short answer: A quick check can catch common problems, but it does not certify the entire page.

    What to do

    1. Open the Canvas page, choose Edit, and select a section title. In the Rich Content Editor, use the heading-format menu to assign a real heading (often Heading 2 for main sections and Heading 3 for subsections). Do not use bold or font size alone as a heading.
    2. Read each link without the surrounding sentence. Replace “click here” with a descriptive name such as “Week 3 guided notes.”
    3. Check images for meaningful alternative text; mark purely decorative images as decorative. Check tables for headers and simple reading order.
    4. Run the Canvas Rich Content Editor accessibility checker, correct its findings, then review the published page. For equations, videos, and embedded tools, use the additional guides below.

    If you need more help: If a page contains complex math, diagrams, or embedded content you cannot verify, ask your campus accessibility team for help.

    Can I post handwritten guided notes as a PDF?

    Short answer: Yes, you may continue using handwritten instruction, but a scanned or image-only PDF by itself can create access barriers. A separate alternative does not automatically make the original web content compliant.

    What to do

    1. Open the PDF and try selecting a sentence. If it is just an image, screen-reader users may not be able to read the text. Selectable text is only an initial check, not proof of accessibility.
    2. Identify what your notes actually teach: equations, worked examples, arrows, highlighted relationships, and diagrams. An accessible version must preserve that essential information and learning activity.
    3. Where possible, create an accessible source document (for example, structured Word equations or accessible Canvas HTML), then test the version students receive. Retain your handwritten notes as a supplemental resource where appropriate.
    4. If you cannot remediate the notes, contact campus accessibility support promptly to arrange effective access and determine how the Title II web requirements apply. A textbook link or transcript is not automatically an adequate substitute.

    If you need more help: Ask whether students have an equally effective opportunity to learn, participate, and complete the same essential work—without unnecessary delay. This is an access check, not an automatic legal exception.

    Are my video captions and transcripts sufficient?

    Short answer: Automatic captions are a starting point, not a guarantee of accessibility.

    What to do

    1. Play a representative section with captions turned on. Correct errors in terminology, names, numbers, and mathematical expressions.
    2. Check that captions match the spoken content and timing. A transcript is useful for review, but it is not automatically a replacement for required captions.
    3. Watch with the sound off, then listen without looking. If a graph, calculation, gesture, or other essential visual information is never communicated, add appropriate spoken description or arrange an accessible approach.

    If you need more help: Use your video platform’s caption editor, and contact media/accessibility support if the needed description or correction is beyond your tools.

    How do I tell whether a YouTube video has automatic captions?

    Short answer: YouTube labels some tracks “auto-generated.” An unlabeled track is not proof that a human reviewed it.

    What to do

    1. Open the YouTube video and select the Settings gear → Subtitles/CC (menu names may vary by device).
    2. Look for a language followed by “auto-generated.” That track was created by automatic speech recognition. If the label is absent, the track may be creator-supplied or edited, but its accuracy is not certified.
    3. Turn captions on and sample the beginning, middle, and a math-heavy portion. Compare captions to the speech: check numbers, signs, variable names, symbols, terminology, and timing.
    4. Listen without watching. If essential board work, graphs, or gestures are not explained in audio, captions alone do not resolve that visual barrier.

    If captions are inaccurate and you do not own the video: You generally cannot edit its YouTube captions. Look for a verified accessible version, contact the creator, or work with campus media/accessibility support on a timely solution. Do not assume auto-generated captions meet accessibility requirements.

    How do I edit captions on a YouTube video I own?

    Short answer: Use YouTube Studio → Subtitles to correct and publish a caption track. Automatic captions should be checked rather than trusted as-is.

    What to do

    1. Sign in to YouTube Studio.
    2. In the left menu, select Subtitles and choose your video.
    3. Find the language/caption track and choose Edit. For an automatically generated track, select Duplicate and edit and continue if that option appears.
    4. Correct the caption text, especially mathematical vocabulary, numbers, signs, and names. Adjust timing where necessary so captions match the speech.
    5. Select Publish. Reopen the public video, turn captions on, and verify the published track in a math-heavy section.

    Remember: Accurate captions convey speech and meaningful audio, but do not automatically communicate silent diagrams, board writing, or demonstrations. Provide appropriate spoken description or seek media accessibility support.

    Can AI help me correct captions for a math or statistics video?

    Short answer: Yes. AI can help identify transcription mistakes and preserve mathematical terminology, but a faculty member must compare corrections with the audio, verify timing, and check essential visual content separately. AI cannot certify ADA or WCAG compliance.

    Copy the math and statistics caption-editing prompt

    How do I make my Word document accessible?

    Short answer: Start with Word’s built-in structure and checker; you do not need to learn HTML.

    What to do

    1. Use Home → Styles to mark section headings as Heading 1, Heading 2, etc., according to the document structure; bold text alone is not a heading.
    2. Use Insert → Equation for mathematical expressions instead of screenshots of equations. Add meaningful alt text to instructional images.
    3. Choose Review → Check Accessibility (the location may vary by Word version). Address flagged issues and review tables and links.
    4. If you export to PDF, check the exported PDF separately: accessibility does not automatically carry over perfectly.

    If you need more help: For complex mathematics, verify the actual file with a screen reader or request support.

    Can I post a scanned PDF?

    Short answer: An image-only scanned PDF generally cannot be read as text by a screen reader without remediation.

    What to do

    1. Try selecting and copying a sentence. If you cannot, the page may be image-only.
    2. If you have the original Word or other editable source, improve that source first and export a tagged PDF where supported.
    3. If scanning is unavoidable, OCR can recognize text, but you still need to check reading order, headings, tables, images, and mathematical expressions.
    4. Test the final PDF with appropriate tools and assistive technology; request remediation support if needed.

    If you need more help: An accessible HTML or source document may be a useful delivery option, but the original PDF and any alternate-version approach still need to be evaluated under applicable requirements.

    I have a PDF. How do I check its accessibility?

    Start here: Do you have the original editable file (such as Word, PowerPoint, or a LaTeX source)? Choose the path that matches your situation. In either case, test the final PDF that students will receive.

    Choose your starting point

    Yes — I have the original editable file
    1. Check the source: In Word, use Home → Styles for real headings, Insert → Equation for structured math, and Review → Check Accessibility (menus vary by version). Check links, tables, figures, and graph descriptions manually.
    2. Fix the source first: Correct headings, table structure, alt text, reading order, and mathematical content where editing is easiest.
    3. Export an accessible PDF: Choose a PDF export method that preserves document structure tags. Do not assume Print to PDF preserves tags.
    4. Check the exported PDF: Run Acrobat Pro's accessibility checker or PAC, inspect tags and reading order, and manually verify equations, diagrams, and screen-reader usability. Fix and re-export or remediate as needed.

    Why both? Passing Word's accessibility checker does not guarantee the exported PDF is accessible.

    No — I have only the PDF
    1. Test the text layer: Select a sentence, copy it, and paste it into Notepad. If nothing selects, it may be an image-only scan. If the text is garbled or out of order, investigate encoding and reading order.
    2. Run a PDF checker: Use Adobe Acrobat Pro's accessibility checker or the free Windows PAC checker. Review reported tags, document title, language, images, and structural issues.
    3. Inspect what the checker misses: Read headings in order; inspect tables, graphs, mathematical notation, links, and color contrast. Verify that a screen-reader user can understand the essential mathematics.
    4. Choose a repair route: For a text-based PDF, repair tags, reading order, and other issues in a capable PDF editor. For scanned pages, OCR is only the first step; check the recognized text and rebuild structure and math. For complex or badly damaged PDFs, reconstruct the content in accessible Word or HTML, or ask campus accessibility specialists for help.
    5. Retest the final student-facing file: An OCR layer or a passing automated report is not a compliance certificate.

    Quick manual review

    • Can I select and copy the text correctly?
    • Are headings, tables, and reading order meaningful?
    • Are graphs and diagrams described adequately?
    • Are equations and symbols understandable with assistive technology?
    • Do links, color contrast, and zoom work for the student's task?

    For math and statistics: A PDF can look perfect and have selectable text while exponents, fractions, symbols, or graph relationships remain inaccessible. Test actual student tasks, not only checker scores.

    Another option: When PDF is not essential, well-structured accessible HTML may work better for complex mathematics; confirm that any replacement meets the instructional need and applicable accessibility requirements.

    When may I post handwritten guided notes?

    Short answer: Handwriting is not prohibited. But posting an image-only handwritten PDF as the sole means of accessing essential instruction can create serious barriers. There is no automatic exemption because notes are supplemental or an alternative exists.

    1. Identify the role: Are the notes required, used to complete assignments, or genuinely optional? What unique equations, annotations, worked examples, and diagrams do they contain?
    2. Check the file: Is its text and mathematical meaning accessible to assistive technology? Selectable text alone is not sufficient.
    3. Prefer an accessible source: Provide structured text, properly authored equations, and descriptions of essential visual annotations. You may keep the handwritten version alongside accessible content, subject to applicable requirements.
    4. If you cannot fix it: Arrange timely access with campus accessibility support and determine how the original posting must be handled. A textbook chapter or generic transcript may omit the actual learning task.

    Screen reader testing for beginners: a 10-minute faculty guide

    Start here if a guide says “test with a screen reader.” You do not need to buy software. A screen reader speaks page content and exposes headings, links, controls, and other structure. This quick check helps you identify barriers; it does not certify accessibility.

    Windows: start with NVDA (recommended for deeper testing)

    Download free NVDA from NV Access. Install and launch it, then open your textbook in Chrome or Firefox. In NVDA's browser browse mode, press H to jump to the next heading and K to jump to the next link; Shift with either key moves backward. Use the Up/Down arrows to read through text. Press NVDA + Q to exit (the NVDA key is commonly Insert; Caps Lock can be configured).

    No installation? Try Windows Narrator

    Press Windows + Ctrl + Enter to start or stop Narrator. Follow the built-in tutorial or Microsoft's Narrator basics. Narrator's navigation commands differ from NVDA's; do not assume the single-letter NVDA shortcuts work in Narrator.

    Mac: use built-in VoiceOver

    Press Command + F5 to start or stop VoiceOver (some keyboards also require Fn), or go to System Settings → Accessibility → VoiceOver. Open your textbook in Safari. The VoiceOver modifier is usually Control + Option (VO). Use VO + Right Arrow to move through items and VO + Left Arrow to move back. VoiceOver's Quick Nav and rotor offer other navigation options.

    Apple's VoiceOver getting-started guide.

    Try this 10-minute check on a real chapter or assignment

    1. Start and orient yourself: Open a representative chapter containing headings, links, equations, and a graph. Turn on your screen reader using the instructions above.
    2. Find headings and links: Move between headings and links. Can you understand the organization and destination names without looking at the screen?
    3. Read one worked example: Listen to a fraction, exponent, equation, or table. Does the spoken output preserve the mathematical meaning, or does it skip symbols or read them in a confusing order?
    4. Try one real task: Use the keyboard to reach an answer field, enter a response, request a hint, and hear feedback. Tab moves between many controls; screen-reader reading commands are different from keyboard-only interaction.
    5. Record what happened: Note the page URL, browser, screen reader and version, exact task, and the words or behavior that caused confusion. Repeat after changes.
    If it doesn't work: Don't assume the student can work around it. Record the barrier and contact campus accessibility or disability services. For vendor-controlled platforms, share the exact activity and request remediation or an accessible way to complete the essential task. See the resolution pathway.

    Important limits: A short faculty test is a useful first screen, not a substitute for experienced assistive-technology users or campus review. Complex math, dynamic graphs, and publisher activities often need specialist testing. Never ask a student to disclose a disability to obtain generally accessible course content.

    Short answer: Identify the blocked learning task, repair or replace the material where possible, retest, and involve institutional support when the barrier remains.

    1. Graph alt text 5 min

    Check: Is the information needed to understand the graph available without seeing it?

    1. On the assigned page, right-click a graph and choose Inspect. In Chrome DevTools, select the image or graphic and open the Accessibility pane. Review its computed name and description and where each comes from. A caption does not automatically become an accessible description.

    MathML, MathJax, LaTeX, and Word equations — what's the difference?

    Word Equation Editor: creates native, structured Office Math equations (OMML); no MathJax required. LaTeX: a notation used to write mathematics, which needs an appropriate renderer or conversion in the final format. MathJax: a web rendering system that can expose accessible mathematical information. MathML: structured markup for mathematics that browsers and assistive technologies may use.

    Not a requirement: WCAG does not prescribe MathJax or MathML for every equation. A MathML code view is a useful diagnostic, not proof of screen-reader accessibility. Test the actual Word document, Canvas page, or exported PDF.

    Faculty scenarios · Individualized access

    Supporting a student with a disability

    Start with the barrier, not the label. A diagnosis or disability category does not tell you which technology, format, or accommodation a particular student uses. Do not ask a student to disclose a diagnosis. Follow the approved accommodation process, communicate respectfully with the student about access, and coordinate promptly with your campus disability services office.

    I received an accommodation letter. What should I do first?

    1. Review the approved accommodations and any implementation instructions; keep the information confidential.
    2. Identify upcoming barriers in lectures, notes, equations, graphs, videos, discussions, quizzes, and third-party tools. Prioritize anything due soon.
    3. Contact disability services promptly when you need help arranging accessible formats, interpreters, captioning, tactile graphics, or technology.
    4. Communicate with the student about how to access course activities without requesting medical details or making assumptions about preferred tools.
    5. Verify the actual activity works and follow up if a barrier remains. Do not wait for a technical fix while a student lacks access.

    Privacy: Share accommodation information only with people who need it to implement approved support, following campus procedures.

    Choose a scenario

    These are examples of possible barriers and supports—not a list of accommodations automatically assigned by disability category.

    A student is blind or uses a screen reader

    Possible barriers: Image-only PDFs, equations that are read as disconnected symbols, unlabeled graphs, visual-only board work, drag-and-drop activities.

    Try: Structured accessible notes; tested mathematical notation; meaningful graph descriptions; keyboard-operable activities; descriptions of information written or pointed to during class.

    Coordinate: Accessible math formats, Braille or tactile graphics when needed, and assistive-technology testing with disability services.

    Math example: For a graph of two functions, convey their equations, intersections, and key behavior—not merely “two curves.”

    Check equations · Check PDFs · Check interactive apps

    A student has low vision or is legally blind

    Possible barriers: Tiny graph labels, thin lines, low contrast, fixed-size diagrams, and PDFs that cannot be comfortably enlarged.

    Try: Scalable content, readable labels, sufficient contrast, text alternatives, and non-color cues such as line patterns or direct labels. Test zoom and reflow where applicable.

    Coordinate: Ask disability services about approved accessible formats and technology; some students use magnification, some use speech or Braille, and some use combinations.

    Math example: Label “Function A” and “Function B” directly or use different line styles rather than relying only on red and green curves.

    Check color contrast · Test a webpage

    A student is deaf or hard of hearing

    Possible barriers: Uncaptioned or inaccurately captioned lectures, spoken explanations of equations, discussion without communication access, and audio-only instructions.

    Try: Accurate synchronized captions for prerecorded videos, accessible written instructions, and verbal references that match visible notation. Make important audio information available in an appropriate text or visual form.

    Coordinate: Disability services can help arrange qualified interpreting, real-time captioning (CART), assistive listening systems, or other approved communication supports. A transcript alone does not necessarily replace synchronized captions or live communication access.

    Math example: Correct auto-captions that turn “x squared minus four” into an unrelated phrase; ensure the graph or equation being discussed is clear.

    Decide what to do next

    What is an academically equivalent substitute?

    Working definition: An alternative instructional material, activity, or assessment that enables students to achieve the same essential learning objectives as the original, with comparable academic rigor, opportunities to practice, feedback, and assessment. For accessibility purposes, it must also support timely and effective access, including independent and private participation where appropriate.

    The substitute need not look or work exactly like the original, but it should preserve the essential educational experience. Academic equivalence alone does not establish ADA or WCAG compliance. Consult campus accessibility specialists about applicable requirements and acceptable solutions.

    Can the original be repaired or replaced before students need it?

    Repair or replace it, then test the actual student task. For PDFs, prioritize the editable source and verify the final export.

    Does the alternative teach and assess the same essential mathematics?

    Check the equations, graph meaning, annotations, practice, interaction, feedback, and timing. A textbook chapter may not replace a guided-note activity; a transcript may not explain a drawn graph. Check independent, private, timely access as applicable.

    Is the barrier in a publisher tool, Canvas feature, or complex media?

    Record the affected URL or file, exact task, observed failure, impact, and deadline. Contact the campus accessibility/IT/procurement team and vendor; ask who owns the fix and how students will have access meanwhile.

    Does an accessible alternative make the original legally acceptable?

    Not automatically. The ADA Title II web rule has specific requirements and limited provisions for conforming alternate versions and exceptions. The institution must assess the applicable requirements, while effective-communication and nondiscrimination duties continue.
